    Nitrogen-fixing crops

    Beans, peas, groundnut and other legumes host root bacteria that take nitrogen from the air. The next crop needs less fertilizer.

    Cover crops

    Grown to protect soil rather than to sell. They keep roots in the ground between cash crops, cut erosion and add organic matter.

    Deep-rooting crops

    Sunflower, sorghum and alfalfa break compacted layers and pull up water and nutrients that shallow roots miss, so rain soaks in better.

    Drought-tolerant crops

    Millet, sorghum, chickpea and cassava yield on less water. Adding one lowers the whole rotation's water demand in dry years.

    Terms explained

    The science, in plain words.

    NDVI

    A satellite measure of how green and dense plants are, from 0 (bare soil) to about 0.9 (thick healthy crop). A falling value can mean stress, harvest or bare ground.

    Evapotranspiration

    Water leaving the field — evaporating from soil plus the water plants breathe out. Hot, dry, windy days raise it, so crops need more water.

    Root-zone wetness

    How full the soil is with water where roots grow, from 0 (dry) to 1 (saturated). This page uses NASA POWER's GWETROOT value.

    Soil moisture anomaly

    How much wetter or drier the soil is than normal for this time of year. Below normal for weeks is an early drought sign.

    Water balance

    Rain coming in minus water going out through evapotranspiration and runoff. A negative balance means the crop is drawing down stored soil water.

    Frost risk

    The chance the air drops to 0 °C or below. One frost can kill tender crops such as tomato or beans; cold air also pools in low spots.

    Extreme rainfall day

    A day wetter than 90% of rainy days in this place's own record. These days cause most erosion and wash nitrogen out of the soil.

    Satellite vs. ground data

    Satellites see everywhere but in coarse pixels (250 m to 50 km here). A soil probe or rain gauge is exact but covers one spot. Using both gives the truest picture.
